Fire behaves differently when nothing rises. Every safety rule written for Earth deserves a second look in orbit, on the Moon, and on Mars.

The science in 3 steps

One change in gravity. Three changes in the fire story.

The key is not that fire stops working. The flow that feeds a flame changes, and that changes what crews need to understand about ignition, spread, smoke, and suppression.

      Physics engine: why the flame has that colour

      Every output on this page is produced by the same small engine. Temperatures come from the sourced values in the Excel data, interpolated with a stated model. The spectrum is built from emitters whose wavelengths come from the Flame Physics sheets. It is then converted to a colour with the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions. Relative emitter strengths are model parameters, so treat the spectrum as an explanation, not a measurement.

      Crew health · before any explosion

      How a cabin fire harms a crew long before anything explodes

      This section is about cabin materials, not rocket propellants. Two hazards come first: smoke particles that the detector must catch, and toxic gases that the crew breathes. The numbers below are copied from NASA papers about the SAME and SAME-R smoke experiments and about combustion-product testing. Each figure has a source ID.

      1 · Smoke particle size from overheated spacecraft materialsPublished research

      Geometric mean diameter (Dg) in the SAME-2 baseline runs on the space station, fresh smoke and after 720 s of ageing. Dashed line: 1,000 nm (1 µm), the size where ISS light-scattering detectors become most sensitive.

      Fresh smokeAged 720 s

      Source R1, Table 1. Diameter repeatability ±15%. Geometric standard deviation ranged 1.834 to 2.530. Teflon is the one material whose Dg did not grow with ageing.

      2 · Does microgravity change the smoke?Published research

      Space Shuttle STS-75 smoke experiment, same magnification, low gravity against 1 g. Primary particle diameter in nanometres.

      1 gLow gravity

      3 · Detector fit explorerCalculated

      Pick a material. The marker shows where its SAME-2 particle size sits against the 1 µm line that the sources use to separate the two detector types.

      Rule from sources R1 and R2: ionization detectors respond better to sub-micron smoke, ISS near-infrared forward-scatter detectors respond most to particles above 1 µm. The comparison here is a reading of the source, not a detector model.

      4 · Toxic gases from heated cabin materialsPublished research

      Carbon monoxide in a 623 L chamber at 12.4 psia, 0.5 g of material, smouldering with a 640 °C furnace and no flame. Mean over runs, in parts per million.

      MaterialCO ppmHCN ppmHF

      Source R4. Kapton gave the most HCN and PTFE essentially none. Fluorine-bearing materials gave the most hydrogen fluoride, which stayed in the chamber for more than two hours in one run. A PTFE/PVC blend (90/10) gave about 25 to 29 ppm HCl, read from an ambiguous table, so treat that figure as approximate.

      5 · The oxygen question for Moon and Mars habitatsPublished research

      NASA’s exploration atmosphere working group recommended 8.2 psia with 34% oxygen as a capability for future missions. Higher oxygen lets crews skip long prebreathe, but fires burn more readily. This site’s earlier wording of “30%+” is the 34% figure here; the source does not name lunar bases specifically.

      Sources R5 and R6. R6 reports that lunar gravity appears to raise flammability limits for some materials against Earth gravity, and that SIBAL fabric burns only downward in air in lunar gravity while on Earth it goes out immediately after ignition. The FM2 experiment is planned to test this on the Moon.

      What this data does and does not show. The sources measure particle size and gas concentration. They do not measure health effects. Whether a given concentration harms a crew depends on exposure time and NASA’s Spacecraft Maximum Allowable Concentration limits, which this page does not reproduce. Check those limits in NASA’s own standard before drawing a conclusion. Two figures in R1 disagree: the zero-airflow particles are called “30% to 40% larger” in one section and “2 to 3 times larger” in the conclusions. R2 lists 100 to 200 nm arithmetic mean diameters for all materials while giving 227 nm for silicone rubber. Both are shown as the sources state them.

        Prevention: what investigators changed, and a workbench for testing your own fix

        136 prevention measures drawn from 14 accident investigations, each linked to the failure mode it addresses and graded for evidence. Explore the record, see where it is thin, then build and stress-test a control plan for your own system. Everything runs in the page; nothing is uploaded.

        Read before using the data
        • Status matters. “Official recommendation”, “Operator action (reported)” and “Analyst-derived” are different kinds of claim. Filter on status before quoting a measure as official guidance.
        • For analyst-derived controls the evidence grade refers to the documented finding they come from, not to the control itself.
        • Implementation was not verified unless a measure is marked as an operator action. A recommendation in this record is not proof that it was carried out.
        • Counts show how many measures the sources record. They do not rank importance, and an empty cell is a gap in the record, not proof that no control existed.

                Questions people ask

                Is this a NASA website?+

                No. It is an independent NASA Space Apps 2026 project. It uses public descriptions of NASA research and is not endorsed by NASA.

                Is the flame animation real data?+

                No. It is an illustrative particle sketch that shows a trend. Real flame images and measurements live in NASA’s published work.

                Why does flame shape change in microgravity?+

                Without buoyancy, hot gas no longer rises and draws air in. Oxygen reaches the flame mainly by diffusion, so flames tend to be rounder and dimmer.

                Where can I verify the investigations?+

                Search each investigation name in NASA’s research and technical report archives. Treat the cards here as a starting point, never as a citation.

                Where do the numbers in the scale chart come from?+

                The curve is v = √(g·L) with g in m/s² and L the flame height you choose. The 1 cm/s reference comes from the BASS II summary. No experimental data points are plotted, because none have been checked against a published paper yet.
